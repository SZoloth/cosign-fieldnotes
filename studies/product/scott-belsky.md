## Decisions and tradeoffs

The source supports the documented observation or reported choice. Proposed alternatives, inferred costs, and transfer questions are editorial synthesis unless explicitly attributed.

### 1. Choose a useful unit of discovery before chasing scale

Content structure and manual onboarding can jointly address an empty-network problem. Belsky recalls manually setting up early portfolios and organizing discovery around projects. Each portfolio supplied multiple browsable examples, making the network useful sooner. He reports subsequent organic publishing growth. This is a retrospective, not a causal experiment isolating the project format.

**Tradeoff:** Human curation creates an initial quality bar but cannot substitute indefinitely for repeatable contribution.

**Limit:** Do not infer that project-first browsing works for every marketplace.

**Transfer question / exercise:** Identify the smallest valuable discovery unit in a new directory. Seed a small set with permission and test whether visitors understand value before asking them to contribute.

Evidence: [Source 1](https://mercury.com/blog/scott-belsky)

Decision ID: `belsky-seed-network`

### 2. Treat first use as part of the product, not launch packaging

New users lack the context that makes a mature team's interface feel obvious. In the Intercom interview, Belsky examines onboarding, copy, defaults and the first action as a connected experience. He also argues that portfolios should reveal a problem and contribution, not only polished fragments. A reasoned principle illustrated by experience, not a universal conversion benchmark.

**Tradeoff:** Reducing initial choices can help orientation while hiding flexibility needed later.

**Limit:** Expert products may need role-specific entry paths; simplistic onboarding can obstruct returning users.

**Transfer question / exercise:** Run a first-use test with no explanation. Record the participant's first prediction, first action and first uncertainty; revise one default, then retest.

Evidence: [Source 1](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/scott-belsky-behance-benchmark)

Decision ID: `belsky-entry`

### 3. Revisit the workflow instead of decorating it with AI

A new capability can remove an old step rather than add an assistant beside it. Belsky's platform-shift essay asks leaders to return to customer needs and consider a new interface over existing data and workflows. He contrasts incremental additions with a more fundamental rethink. An argument with examples, not verified evidence that an overlay produces superior retention.

**Tradeoff:** A new layer may unify work, but can also introduce another interface and duplicate existing controls.

**Limit:** His investments and advisory roles are relevant context; treat forecasts as hypotheses.

**Transfer question / exercise:** Draw the current workflow and a version with half the steps. For each removed step, state what new capability replaces it and what trust or control must remain.

Evidence: [Source 1](https://www.implications.com/p/the-data-wars-and-reimagining-your)

Decision ID: `belsky-reimagine`

### 4. Separate durable context from replaceable screens

Company-specific knowledge may remain valuable even when interfaces become easier to build. The 2026 essay argues that internal teams can create tailored software and that organizational relationships and accumulated context can outlast particular applications. A forecast, not demonstrated superiority of custom software.

**Tradeoff:** Tailoring improves fit but transfers maintenance, security and integration responsibilities to the organization.

**Limit:** Total lifecycle cost and governance can make a standardized product the better choice.

**Transfer question / exercise:** Write a build-versus-buy memo separating unique workflow knowledge from commodity features. Include a three-year maintenance owner and exit strategy.

Evidence: [Source 1](https://www.implications.com/p/5-things-top-product-leaders-love)

Decision ID: `belsky-company-specific`

## Communication observations

These are observations about the linked written or spoken explanation. Interview structure and edited summaries may also reflect the publisher's work.

### 1. Use a physical analogy to expose an obsolete assumption

The platform-shift essay uses the disappearance of toll-booth friction to make workflow removal imaginable. Choose an analogy that clarifies one mechanism, then state where it stops matching your product.

[Linked example](https://www.implications.com/p/the-data-wars-and-reimagining-your)

### 2. Separate observation from implication

The tailored-software essay starts from current tool changes and advances a larger forecast. Its explicit speculative framing is important to retain. Label each paragraph as observed, inferred or predicted before sharing a strategy memo.

[Linked example](https://www.implications.com/p/5-things-top-product-leaders-love)

### 3. Explain why the artifact's shape mattered

The Behance recollection connects project-sized portfolios to discovery density rather than merely announcing that the network grew. Connect a design choice to its proposed mechanism, then distinguish that mechanism from proven causality.

[Linked example](https://mercury.com/blog/scott-belsky)

## Practice

Original exercises inspired by the sources, not practitioner-prescribed methods.

### 1. Write a falsifiable interface thesis

Identify one workflow whose assumptions changed. Separate stable user need from historical tool constraints. Propose a simpler entry point. Name a measurable result and an observation that would invalidate the thesis. Describe the maintenance and trust obligations created by the new interface.

**Deliverable:** A two-page memo and a first-use prototype.

**Evaluate:** The proposal can fail a concrete test; it does not depend only on agreement with a trend.
